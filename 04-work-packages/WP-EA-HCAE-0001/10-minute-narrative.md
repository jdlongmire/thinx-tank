# Ten-Minute Narrative — Enterprise Architecture in the GenAI Era

Audience: developing enterprise architect / protégé  
Target duration: approximately 10 minutes  
Tone: conversational mentoring

## 0:00–1:30 — What Enterprise Architecture actually is

When people first encounter Enterprise Architecture, they often see the artifacts: diagrams, standards, reference architectures, capability maps, technology roadmaps. Those are useful, but they are outputs of the discipline.

The real job is maintaining intentional coherence as an enterprise changes.

Leadership establishes strategy. Strategy tells us where the enterprise intends to go and why. But a strategy does not implement itself. It has consequences for business capabilities, information, applications, infrastructure, cybersecurity, organizations, investment, and delivery.

Enterprise Architecture works across those boundaries.

So I want you to think about a simple chain:

**Strategy → Architecture → Roadmaps → Standards → Delivery.**

Strategy establishes intent. Architecture translates that intent into a coherent future state and the constraints necessary to reach it. Roadmaps sequence the change. Standards prevent every program from independently solving the same problems. Delivery turns the architecture into an operational enterprise.

The architect's responsibility is coherence across that chain.

## 1:30–3:00 — The architect deals in relationships

Suppose leadership announces a new strategic objective.

A solution architect can ask, "What system should I build?"

The enterprise architect has to ask a larger set of questions.

What enterprise capabilities does this objective require? Which capabilities already exist? Which must change? What information do they depend upon? Which systems implement them? Which organizations own them? What standards constrain them? Which initiatives are already changing them? Where are we duplicating investment? Where will a local optimization create an enterprise problem?

That is why EA becomes difficult at scale.

The challenge is rarely a shortage of documents. Enterprises usually have too many. The difficulty is maintaining the relationships among thousands of facts, decisions, constraints, systems, capabilities, organizations, and initiatives while all of them are changing.

Historically, architects have spent enormous amounts of time finding and reconciling that information before they can reason about it.

That is where GenAI becomes interesting.

## 3:00–5:00 — What GenAI changes

Imagine that the enterprise's authoritative architecture knowledge is available to an AI-enabled capability.

Now I can ask:

"What capabilities are affected by this strategic objective?"

"Show me the systems implementing those capabilities."

"Which standards constrain the proposed architecture?"

"What initiatives are already modifying those systems?"

"Where does this proposal conflict with an approved architecture decision?"

"What assumptions in this roadmap are unsupported?"

"Generate three candidate architecture viewpoints for this decision and show me the evidence behind each."

Those are tasks that used to require hours or days of searching, correlating, reading, and assembling information.

GenAI can compress that work dramatically.

It can ingest approved artifacts, normalize terminology, discover relationships, summarize decisions, identify inconsistencies, generate candidate viewpoints, draft architecture decision records, compare proposed solutions against standards, and surface questions an architect may need to investigate.

That changes the leverage of an architect.

It does not eliminate the architect.

## 5:00–7:00 — Human-Curated, AI-Enabled

This distinction is critical.

I call the operating principle **Human-Curated, AI-Enabled**, or HCAE.

The human architect determines what is authoritative. The human understands why a standard exists. The human decides whether two apparently similar concepts actually mean the same thing. The human understands organizational context, mission consequences, risk tolerance, and when an exception is justified.

The AI can correlate evidence. It can identify patterns. It can generate alternatives. It can challenge consistency. It can accelerate analysis.

But generation is not authority.

And human curation is not simply clicking "approve" after the machine has done the work.

Curation happens throughout the lifecycle. Humans select authoritative sources. They establish semantics. They resolve conflicts. They set policy. They define acceptance criteria. They test important claims. They reject bad machine output. They make consequential decisions and remain accountable for them.

Think of human judgment and governance as the control plane.

AI is an extraordinarily powerful capability operating underneath that control plane.

## 7:00–8:30 — From documents to a living architecture

This leads to a deeper change in how I think architecture will be practiced.

Today, architecture is often represented as a collection of documents.

A strategy document lives here. A capability model lives somewhere else. Application inventories are in another system. Standards are PDFs. Roadmaps are PowerPoint slides. Architecture decisions are buried in meeting notes.

The enterprise knows all of these things, but it does not necessarily know them together.

An AI-enabled architecture capability gives us an opportunity to treat architecture as a living enterprise knowledge system.

Imagine traversing:

**Strategic Objective → Capability → Architecture Element → Roadmap Initiative → Standard → Decision → Implementation → Evidence.**

Now architecture becomes queryable.

More importantly, it becomes traceable.

When someone proposes a technical change, we can trace upward and ask what strategic purpose it serves. When leadership changes strategy, we can trace downward and ask what architectures, roadmaps, standards, investments, and implementations are affected.

That is much closer to what Enterprise Architecture was always trying to accomplish.

## 8:30–9:30 — What remains uniquely your responsibility

So as you develop as an architect, do not focus only on learning how to produce architecture artifacts.

Learn why the enterprise is structured the way it is.

Learn to distinguish a requirement from an assumption.

Learn to recognize local optimization.

Learn how strategy becomes capability and how capability becomes implementation.

Learn governance without becoming bureaucratic.

Learn when a standard protects enterprise coherence and when it has become obsolete.

Learn to ask what evidence would change your conclusion.

Those are judgment skills.

Then learn GenAI well enough to automate and accelerate the information work surrounding that judgment.

## 9:30–10:00 — The opportunity

That is the opportunity I see for your generation of architects.

Traditionally, an architect might spend three weeks assembling information so they can spend three hours thinking about it.

I want to invert that ratio.

Let the machine help retrieve, correlate, challenge, synthesize, trace, and present the information.

Spend your time understanding the enterprise, evaluating alternatives, making decisions, communicating consequences, and preserving coherence.

The formula is straightforward:

**Human authority + curated enterprise knowledge + AI leverage = architecture at enterprise scale.**

Learn architecture deeply enough to know what must remain human.

Then learn AI well enough to amplify everything around it.
