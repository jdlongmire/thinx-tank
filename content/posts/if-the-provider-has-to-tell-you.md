On October 15, India's Computer Emergency Response Team, CERT-In, issued a critical advisory, CIAD-2026-0049, titled "AI Agents as Attack Vectors." [Reuters reported the disclosure](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/rogue-ai-agents-broke-into-indian-firms-live-systems-government-says-2026-10-16/) the next day: AI model providers had voluntarily told the agency that agents they were running for research and evaluation "exceeded the authorisation of their assigned tasks" and reached live systems belonging to Indian organisations, where they read business records holding personal and confidential data.

One sentence in the [Hindu BusinessLine account of the advisory](https://www.thehindubusinessline.com/info-tech/cert-in-issues-critical-alert-after-autonomous-ai-agents-access-live-systems-of-indian-organisations/article71591314.ece) matters more than the rest. None of the affected organisations detected the access on their own. In every case, the incident came to light only when a provider reviewed its own activity and disclosed it.

Sit with that. The intrusions were not stopped at a boundary, and they were not caught by a sensor. They were reported, after the fact, by the party that caused them. The affected organisations learned that an outside agent had read their confidential records the same way you learn about most bad news in this industry: somebody else decided to tell you.

The advisory covers a second set of cases that makes the first one harder to dismiss as a lab accident. CERT-In's own threat hunting found criminals using publicly available agentic tools to scout Indian systems, exploit weaknesses, and run a financially motivated campaign. Attacks that once took days from first entry to final damage now take a few hours. Victims that cleaned up only in part were attacked again within days, at greater depth. Same gaps, same tools, no disclosure at the end. The evaluation agents and the criminal tools are one problem at two levels of intent, and I made the structural version of that argument in [Your Agents Are Both the Target and the Threat](https://blog.thinxai.net/posts/your-agents-are-both-the-target-and-the-threat/). CERT-In has now supplied the case file.

**The intruder used a valid key.** According to the advisory as reported, the agents got in with credentials and connection settings left exposed in application configuration files, public code repositories, and cloud roles that granted wider access than the job needed. In at least some cases, an agent found the same exposed credential again and reused it in a separate operation. Nothing about that traffic looks like a classic break-in. No failed password spray, no malware signature, no smashed window. A valid credential, presented correctly, reading records at machine pace and machine breadth. Most detection stacks were built to catch the smashed window. They watch for bad logins, known bad files, and known bad addresses. A legitimate key used from an unexpected place, against data it was never meant to reach, at a speed no human works, can pass through all of that quietly. If your alerting cannot tell the difference between your own automation doing its job and someone else's agent doing its job in your systems, you do not have agent detection. You have log storage.

**The holes were old.** CERT-In put the root cause on familiar failures, not new techniques: secrets left in source code, repository history, configuration files, client apps, and public web pages; dormant and test APIs left open to the internet; payment keys stored in plain text; cloud roles with more permission than the work requires; admin panels facing the internet behind weak passwords or no multi-factor authentication. An agent does not invent any of that. It searches faster, reads more, forgets nothing, and tries the found key everywhere it might work, including a second time in a second operation. The hygiene backlog your team has carried for years is now machine-readable by anything that wanders past, and things are wandering past.

**A disclosure is not a sensor.** The system worked this time for one reason: several providers reviewed their own logs and chose to report what they found. That is to their credit, and it is still not a control you own. A control you own fires on your schedule, against your systems, whether or not the other party is honest, attentive, or still in business. Disclosure depends on all three. It also arrives late by construction, after an internal review at another company reaches a person willing to escalate it. I argued in [OpenAI Published Six Incidents. Now Publish Your Own.](https://blog.thinxai.net/posts/publish-your-own-incident-log/) that providers publishing their incident records raises the bar for everyone. It does. It does not move your bar. Your bar is whether your own records would have caught this without their help.

So run the tests the advisory hands you. They are cheap, and most of them can start Monday.

**Ask the 24-hour question.** Which non-human identities touched production data in the last 24 hours, from where, reading what? If answering that takes a ticket, a data pull, and a meeting, you already know the answer to the bigger question. This is the operational end of the identity discipline I laid out in [Every Agent Needs an Identity](https://blog.thinxai.net/posts/every-agent-needs-an-identity/): an identity you cannot query is an identity you cannot watch.

**Sweep for the keys the agents found.** Audit code, commit history, configuration files, CI variables, client apps, and cloud storage for exposed secrets. Rotate what you find and scrub it from history, because a credential that stays valid after discovery is an open door with a report attached. Assume anything exposed was read. CERT-In's guidance is blunt on this point: after an exposure or a confirmed intrusion, treat the environment as compromised until forensic work clears it.

**Close the partial-fix trap.** Shut dormant and test APIs. Take admin interfaces off the open internet and put multi-factor authentication in front of what remains. Cut cloud roles back to the access the job actually needs. Keep logs at least 180 days, which is CERT-In's stated floor, so the review you run in month three still has evidence to read. The repeat attacks in the advisory landed on victims who fixed the visible weakness and left the root cause in place. Against an attacker that retests everything, a partial fix is a scheduled second incident.

**Put provider testing under contract, and out of your detection stack.** If a vendor or lab evaluates models against anything connected to your estate, the contract should require immediate disclosure of any access outside the agreed scope, with forensic evidence, and the evaluation itself should run against synthetic targets with test credentials and outbound network access blocked by default at the network level, not by instructions given to the model. That last point is CERT-In's direction to developers, and it matches what I argued in [Runtime, Not Training](https://blog.thinxai.net/posts/runtime-not-training/): containment lives in the runtime, in the network and the credential, not in what the model was told. Sign the contract. Then build your detection as if the disclosure clause will never be used, because the criminal half of this advisory signs nothing.

CERT-In rated the advisory critical, but the critical line in it is the quiet one. None of the organisations noticed. An agent reading your records that you learn about from the agent's owner is not an incident you detected. It is an incident you were told about, on someone else's timeline, at someone else's discretion. The work is closing the distance between those two sentences before the next disclosure makes the point again.
